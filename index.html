    The landmark problem and the data

    Suppose you are a data scientist at a photo-mapping startup, asked to automate the categorization of famous landmarks. The dataset is images of 10 famous landmarks downloaded from the web. We train a CNN on them; at inference the model is fed an image (like a visual-search app) and outputs the most probable landmark category.

    Visual (reverse image) search lets you search with an image instead of text, and returns visually similar results or identifies what is in the photo.

    Choosing the metric

    Accuracy = correct predictions / total samples. The test set is tiny (a few dozen images), so each test image moves accuracy by about 2 points: treat small differences with caution. We also use top-k accuracy, explained in the next section.

    Exploring the data first

    The folders are New_landmark_images/{train,validation,test}/<class_name>/. The classes are roughly balanced, with only a few dozen images per class in train, fewer in validation and a handful in test: a small dataset, the typical situation where transfer learning shines.

  02
  
    Why not from scratch? ImageNet and AlexNet
    
  
    There are two ways to classify these landmarks:
    
  
    
    
    1. A CNN from scratch
    How many conv layers? Which architecture? 3×3, 5×5 or 11×11 kernels? How to combine conv, pooling and FC layers? And can a small dataset give high accuracy? This needs a lot of experimentation and training time.
    
    
    2. Transfer learning
    Reuse a state-of-the-art model trained on a huge dataset. We compare both approaches using VGGNet, after first meeting its predecessor, AlexNet.
    
  
    

  
    AlexNet and ImageNet
    
  
    AlexNet won the ImageNet competition (ILSVRC) in 2012 with a CNN, by a large margin: about 84.7% top-5 accuracy versus about 74% for the runner-up. It is widely seen as the start of the deep-learning revolution in vision. CNNs were much older (LeCun et al., 1989); what was missing was compute (GPUs) and large labelled datasets.
    
  
    ImageNet is a database of over 14 million labelled images (over 21,000 categories) organized in a hierarchy. ILSVRC uses a 1000-class subset and became the benchmark for vision models.
    
  
    

  
    Top-k accuracy
    
  
    In many multiclass vision problems, top-k accuracy (top-3, top-5) is a better measure. A recommender system, for instance, shows the 3 or 5 best items rather than only the single best. In top-k accuracy we get a point if the right answer appears in the top k guesses. For images this helps because some classes differ only slightly (a pickup truck vs a minivan) and even human labels are not 100% correct.
    
  
    
  
    Lecture figures: top-k accuracy
    

  
    What was so new about AlexNet?
    
  
    
    
    1
    GPUs
    One of the first times a large CNN was trained successfully on GPUs. Thousands of cores allow parallel computing across many threads.
    
    
    2
    ReLU
    Before 2012, tanh and sigmoid were common. ReLU gives better gradient propagation (fewer vanishing gradients than tanh) and is cheap, with no expensive exponential.
    
    
    3
    Regularization
    Dropout and data augmentation (random 224×224 crops from 256×256 images, horizontal flips, colour PCA jitter) reduced overfitting.
    
    
    4
    Pooling
    Reduces feature-map dimensions and the number of parameters, so the model trains faster.
    
    
    5
    Depth
    More depth improved the model’s learning capacity.
    
  
    

  
    
  
    

  
    The architecture
    
  
    
  
    AlexNet has 5 conv layers and 3 FC layers and about 61 million parameters.
    
  
    

  
    Shortcomings of AlexNet
    
  
      
    
    • It uses large filters (11×11, 5×5), which have many parameters and are expensive.
      • 
    
    • It is relatively shallow. Deeper networks have more expressive power (several successive non-linearities), a larger receptive field built cheaply from small filters (3×3 layers interleaved with pooling can “see” a 128×128 region without a 128×128 filter), and learn a hierarchy of features (edges → textures → parts → objects) instead of memorizing in one wide layer.
      • 
  
    
  
    Can smaller filters give the same effect? Yes: stack 3×3 filters. Stacking gives the same receptive field with fewer parameters, plus extra non-linearities (a ReLU after each conv).
    
  
    OptionReceptive fieldWeights
    
    
    one 5×5 filter5×525
    two stacked 3×3 filters5×518
    
    
    one 7×7 filter7×749
    three stacked 3×3 filters7×727

    VGGNet and the VGG16 build
    
  
    VGG (Oxford’s Visual Geometry Group) was runner-up in classification and winner of localization at ILSVRC 2014. It is similar in spirit to AlexNet but much deeper and uniform: only 3×3 convolutions and 2×2 max-pooling, grouped in 5 blocks, followed by FC layers.

    weight layers: 13 conv + 3 FC
    
    
    VGG19

    weight layers: 16 conv + 3 FC
    
  
    
  
    
  
    

  
    Building VGG16 from scratch
    
  
    Every VGG block is n × (3×3 Conv + ReLU) followed by a 2×2 max-pool, which halves height and width. VGG16 uses (2, 2, 3, 3, 3) convolutions in its five blocks; VGG19 uses (2, 2, 4, 4, 4). Channels grow 64 → 128 → 256 → 512 → 512.
    
  
    To keep training fast, the three big FC layers (25088 → 4096 → 4096 → 1000) are replaced by a single Linear(512·7·7, 10) layer.

  05
  
    What is transfer learning?
    
  
    Why did deep learning take off? Bigger, better datasets such as ImageNet; faster and cheaper GPUs; better algorithms (architectures, optimizers, training procedures); and pretrained models that took weeks to train but can be reused in minutes. The last point is probably one of the biggest reasons for the widespread adoption of deep learning: if every task took weeks, only a handful of well-funded researchers would work in the area. Transfer learning is the underappreciated hero.
    
  
    
  
    A model trained for one task is reused as the starting point for a second, related task. Early layers of a CNN learn generic features (edges, colours, textures); later layers become more task-specific. So we keep the generic layers and replace the task-specific ones: remove the final fully-connected layers, add a new head for our classes, then train.
    
  
    

  
    Two strategies
    
  
    Feature extractionFine-tuning
    
    
    Pretrained backbonefrozen (not updated)some or all layers unfrozen, trained with a small learning rate
    
    
    Trained partonly the new headnew head + part of the backbone
    
    
    Best whenvery little data, task similar to ImageNetmore data and/or a task that differs from ImageNet
    
    
    Riskmay underfit if tasks differ a lotoverfitting; a high learning rate can destroy pretrained features
    
  
    With only a few dozen images per class we start with feature extraction: much less training time and usually much higher accuracy than training from scratch on a small dataset.

  08
  
    VGG shortcomings, GAP and conclusion
    
  
      
    
    • VGG has about 138 to 144 million parameters, about 500 MB to store. This is a major drawback.
      • 
    
    • Most parameters come from the last 3 FC layers (roughly 120 million); the convolutional part has around 15–20 million weights.
      • 
    
    • Conv layers matter more than FC layers, since they capture the features of the image.
      • 
  
    
  
    Global Average Pooling
    
  
    A pooling layer applied over the entire feature map, using the average instead of the maximum. The feature map before VGG’s FC layers is 7 × 7 × 512; every 7×7 map is averaged to a single value, so 7×7×512 → GAP → 1×1×512, which can feed straight into a dense layer. With GAP we can get rid of most of the FC-layer parameters and still get good performance in practice.
    
  
    
  
    
  
    
  
    This gives rise to GoogLeNet (Inception), which uses global average pooling at the end, and later ResNet, which solves the optimization problem of deep networks with skip connections.
    

  
    Conclusion
    
  
    
  
    Training a deep CNN such as VGG from scratch on a small dataset performs poorly. Transfer learning with a pretrained VGG16 (frozen convolutional layers + a new head) trains in minutes and performs far better.
